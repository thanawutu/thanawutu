# Section-by-Section Strategies

Format details below follow the official MU GRAD TEST guidelines; always
cross-check the current guidelines PDF before your test date.

---

## Listening — 20 points

**Format:** ~30 minutes. 4 passages (conversations/short talks on academic or
everyday topics), 5 questions each. **Each passage is played twice.**

**Strategies**

1. **Use the two plays differently.** First play: listen for the big picture —
   topic, speakers' purpose, overall opinion. Second play: hunt for the
   details the questions ask about.
2. **Preview questions** whenever the interface allows it. Knowing that a
   question asks "why" vs. "when" changes what you listen for.
3. **Take minimal notes**: numbers, names, dates, and cause→effect arrows.
   Full sentences make you miss the next line.
4. **Distractors repeat words, answers repeat meaning.** A choice that reuses
   an exact phrase from the audio is often a trap; the correct answer usually
   paraphrases.
5. Never leave a blank — there's no penalty for guessing.

**Practice:** TOEFL ITP listening sets, TED-Ed talks, BBC 6 Minute English →
graduate to full-speed lectures (MIT OpenCourseWare intro lectures).

## Structure (Grammar) — 20 points

**Format:** ~30 minutes. Two parts:
- **Incomplete sentences (10 items):** choose the word/phrase that completes
  the sentence.
- **Written expression (10 items):** four underlined parts; identify the one
  containing an error.

This is nearly the same format as TOEFL ITP "Structure & Written Expression,"
so ITP practice books are the best drill material.

**The 12 highest-yield grammar points**

1. **Subject–verb agreement** — especially with intervening phrases:
   *The results of the study **were**…* (not *was*).
2. **Verb tense consistency** — past narrative stays past; *since* + present
   perfect.
3. **Parallel structure** — items in a list share one grammatical form:
   *reading, writing, and speaking*.
4. **Word form** — noun vs. adjective vs. adverb slot: *an **economic**
   policy*, *grow **economically***.
5. **Articles** — *a/an* with singular countables; *the* for specific/known;
   no article for general plurals and uncountables.
6. **Prepositions after verbs/adjectives** — *depend **on***, *capable
   **of***, *interested **in***.
7. **Relative clauses** — *who* (people), *which* (things), *whose*
   (possession); no double subject: ~~*the man who he came*~~.
8. **Comparatives/superlatives** — *more efficient than*, *the most
   efficient*; never both (~~*more better*~~).
9. **Conditionals** — If + present → will; If + past → would; If + past
   perfect → would have + V3.
10. **Passive voice** — be + V3; check the doer: *the experiment **was
    conducted***.
11. **Gerund vs. infinitive** — *enjoy do**ing***, *decide **to do***,
    *stop doing* vs. *stop to do*.
12. **Countable/uncountable pairs** — *many/much*, *few/little*,
    *number of/amount of*.

**Written-expression tactic:** check the underlined parts in this order —
verb (agreement/tense) → word form → parallelism → preposition/article.
Roughly in that order of frequency.

## Reading — 40 points (the biggest section)

**Format:** ~60 minutes. 5 passages, ~500 words each, from academic
textbooks, journals, magazines, and newspapers written for non-specialist
readers. Questions cover main ideas, details, inference, vocabulary in
context, the writer's view, and rhetorical purpose.

**Time budget:** 12 minutes per passage — set this as a hard rule in
practice. If a question takes more than ~60 seconds, mark, guess, move on.

**Strategies by question type**

- **Main idea:** read the first paragraph and each paragraph's first sentence
  before anything else. Wrong answers are usually *too narrow* (one detail)
  or *too broad*.
- **Detail ("According to the passage…"):** locate the keyword, then read the
  sentence **before and after** it. The correct answer paraphrases; exact
  word matches are bait.
- **Inference ("implied/suggested"):** the answer must be *provable* from the
  text — pick the smallest logical step, not the most interesting claim.
- **Vocabulary in context:** substitute each choice into the sentence; the
  passage's context beats the word's most common meaning.
- **Writer's view / rhetorical purpose:** watch stance markers — *however,
  although, unfortunately, surprisingly, claims (vs. shows)*.

**Building speed (weeks, not days):** daily reading of academic-register
prose + paragraph summarizing (one 5-word summary per paragraph) is what
moves reading speed. Vocabulary size is the other half — see
[vocabulary.md](vocabulary.md).

## Writing — 20 points

**Format:** ~60 minutes, essay task, typed on the computer.

**A reliable 4-paragraph template (~300–350 words)**

1. **Introduction (3–4 sentences):** paraphrase the prompt → state your
   position → preview your two reasons.
2. **Body 1:** topic sentence → explanation → concrete example → link back.
3. **Body 2:** same shape, second reason (or concession + rebuttal:
   *Admittedly, … However, …* — this reads as more sophisticated).
4. **Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** restate position in fresh words +
   broader implication. No new arguments.

**Time split:** 10 min plan → 40 min write → 10 min proofread. The
proofread pass catches exactly the errors the Structure section tests:
subject–verb agreement, tense, articles, word form.

**Score boosters**

- Vary sentence openings (*Although…*, *Given that…*, *One reason…*).
- Use precise academic linkers: *consequently, in contrast, moreover,
  whereas* — but at most one per sentence.
- Concrete examples beat vague ones: "a nurse working double shifts" >
  "some people in some jobs."

**Common Thai-speaker pitfalls to proofread for:** dropped articles
(*a/an/the*), missing plural *-s*, subject–verb agreement, run-on sentences
joined by commas, and literal translations of Thai connectors.

## Speaking — 20 points (MU GRAD PLUS only)

**Format:** computer-based — you speak into a microphone against a timer,
with no human interlocutor.

**Strategies**

1. **Practice the exact condition:** record yourself answering prompts on a
   timer. The awkwardness of talking to a screen fades only with repetition.
2. **Use a universal answer shape:** direct answer → reason → example →
   wrap-up ("...and that's why I think X"). It fills 45–60 seconds naturally.
3. **Fluency beats accent.** Steady pace with few long pauses scores better
   than fast, halting speech. Replace *"umm"* with a short silent pause.
4. **Buy thinking time with stock openers:** "That's an interesting
   question — in my experience, …"
5. Keep grammar simple and correct: reliable present/past simple beats
   broken complex sentences.

**Daily drill (10 min):** one prompt → 1-minute recorded answer → listen
back → note ONE fix → answer the same prompt again applying the fix.
